Decisions

The decisions, without the story wrapped around them.

Every case study on this site is really a sequence of small, specific choices — one thing picked over another, for a stated reason. This page pulls twelve of those out on their own, in case that's a faster way to see how I think than reading four full case studies. Each one links back to where it happened.

Contract Understanding — open case study →

01Structure

A folder tree, or a relationship model

The agreed direction was to improve the folder hierarchy. I argued the hierarchy itself was the problem — contract relationships are many to many, and a tree can't hold that. The direction moved to separating navigation from an explicit relationship model, and it was approved for build.

See the argument
02Layout

A radial map, until it hit 340 contracts

The first layout failed at real scale — zero legible labels across 340 statements of work. The replacement branches instead of encircling: each click opens one column, and no column ever holds more than about thirty records.

See the fix

Auto Extraction — open case study →

03Trust

A confidence score, tried and removed

The default answer to "how sure is the model" is a confidence score. I tried it and took it out — a lawyer can't act on 0.82. The interface shows where a value came from instead, because that's what reviewers were already reading for.

See it
04Review flow

One value at a time, not a wall of 128

Values are shown one at a time instead of all at once. It costs a reviewer some sense of the whole page, and buys a sequence they can work down without deciding what to check next — worth the trade on a hundred-page agreement.

See it
05Review flow

Failures sorted by kind, not lumped together

Corrections are counted by what kind of mistake they were, not as one error total. A value the model missed entirely and a value it got wrong are different failures, and they need different fixes.

See it
06Iteration

Hover cards, tested and dropped

Hover cards looked clean on a single value and fell apart across a whole contract. Two of the three designs at this stage were mine, and both failed — the lesson was to test anything at the volume it will actually run at, not the volume a demo shows.

07Interface

An ask bar instead of a chat reply

Type an instruction — "supplier renewals above £1M this quarter" — and the page itself becomes the result: filtered, retitled, counted. It doesn't answer in a paragraph, because a fluent sentence can be wrong in ways that are hard to catch, and a filtered page is easy to check.

See it
08Honesty

Showing the case where it fails, on purpose

One of the two example journeys is a miss — the system flags nothing because nobody knew the rule mattered until that morning. It's included on purpose. A system that only demos what it already handles is hiding something.

See it
09Measurement

Measures picked before the build

There are no outcome numbers yet. The four measures that will judge this were chosen before the build, specifically because they're hard to fake.

10Research

Call-centre data instead of a guess

The starting point was real call-centre data, not an assumption about what people needed. The reasons customers called were concentrated — 4G speed alone drove over four million calls.

See it
11Entry point

What the problem feels like, not its category name

The page opens with what a person is probably there for, in their own words — "can't use the internet," not a category name — because most people don't know what their issue is officially called.

See it
12Support cost

Reassurance, designed instead of staffed

Half of what a support call buys is reassurance that something is happening. That can be built into a screen instead of paid for with a person on the line.
