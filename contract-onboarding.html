Problem

A review flow with three checkpoints: review what was extracted, review the counterparty mapping, review the contract families. What onboarding is, and why it was slow

Contract software is only useful once the contracts are inside it. For a new customer that means bringing in everything they have already signed, often thousands of files, from shared drives and email folders and previous systems.

Each file has to end up with two things attached. The counterparty: which company is on the other side. And the family: which master agreement it lives under, whether it is an amendment, a statement of work, an exhibit or a side letter. Without those, nothing downstream works. A renewal date on an amendment means nothing if the system does not know which contract it amends.

Historically people did this by reading. Open the file, find the parties, guess the relationship from the title, link it by hand. It is the kind of work that is tedious at fifty documents and a project at five thousand.

An agent can now read all of it and propose both facts for every file. That is where my work starts, because a proposal about who a contract is with is exactly the kind of thing a company cannot let a model decide alone.

The agent can name the party and place the document. The company is still the one who has to be right.

Three checkpoints, not one approval

The obvious design is one screen at the end: here is what the agent did, approve it. I argued against that, because the agent's work happens in stages and an error in an early stage multiplies. If the agent reads the wrong party name from a document, then every family it builds on that name is wrong too, and a person reviewing the finished hierarchy is trying to spot a mistake that has already spread.

So the flow has three stops, in the order the agent works: review extraction, where the values it pulled from each document are checked (this is the Auto Extraction module, its own case study); counterparty mapping, where the names it found are matched to companies the customer already knows; and review families, where documents are grouped under their parent agreements.

Each stop is a place a person can say "yes, go on" quickly when the agent is right, and fix one thing without redoing everything when it is not.

Identify

The agent reads the extracted parties, normalises the names (Ltd, Limited, Inc, spacing and punctuation), and groups documents that share one.

Validate

A person sees each group as one row, with the agent's name beside the customer's own record for that company, and confirms or changes it.

Verify

Only then are families generated, from mappings a person has already stood behind. Unmapped documents are held back rather than guessed.

Who is this contract with?

The counterparty screen is a table with three tabs: Mapped, Missing and Orphaned. Every row is a company, not a document.

That was the first real decision. The agent works document by document, and the first drafts of the screen did too: a long list of files, each with a proposed party. Reviewing it meant reading the same company name over and over. Grouping by the agent's normalised counterparty turns a long list of files into a short list of companies, and each row carries a count: 3 documents, 24 documents. Open a row and you see the members, each with the exact name the agent extracted from that file, so a person can see that "IBM Japan" and "IBM Functions Pvt Ltd" were folded into one and decide whether that was right.

Beside the agent's name sits the system counterparty, the record the customer already holds for that company. When they match, the row is quiet. When the agent had to choose, a reasoning panel explains the match in plain words, with the passage it read and a note on what it ignored to make the match, rather than the match percentage the earlier version carried. That is the same position I took in Auto Extraction: a lawyer cannot act on 0.82, but they can read a sentence and disagree with it.

When the agent finds nobody

The Missing tab holds the groups where the agent read a party name but found no matching company in the customer's records. This is where onboarding used to stall, because someone had to decide, for each one, whether this was a company they knew under a different name or a new one to create.

The screen offers both, in that order. Opening the empty system-counterparty cell shows a short recommended list first, the closest existing records, and only below that the option to create a new supplier. Picking wrong here creates a duplicate company that will confuse every report afterwards, so the design leans toward reuse.

For large batches there is Bulk Create: create every missing counterparty from the agent's normalised names in one action. I treated this as the most consequential control on the screen. It is the last moment a person can stop the agent's naming from becoming the customer's master data, so it asks for a review of the grouped names first, with a revert on any group the agent merged, before the confirm.

Where does it belong?

Once the counterparties are settled, the agent generates families: for each company, which document is the root, which are parents, and which amendments, statements of work, exhibits and certificates hang under them.

The review screen is a tree per counterparty, with three numbers always visible at the top: how many families, how many documents, how many orphaned. That last number is the one I fought to keep on screen. An orphan is a document the agent could not place, and the easiest way to make onboarding look finished is to hide them. Keeping the count next to the tree means nobody can export a hierarchy without knowing what was left out of it.

Correcting the tree is direct: drag a document to a different parent. Documents are typed as contractual, peripheral or supporting, so a statement of work and a work-completion certificate do not look like the same kind of thing even when they sit at the same level.

This is the screen where the contract relationships argument stopped being an argument. That case study says a repository should describe how contracts relate rather than where files sit. This is the shipped place where those relationships get built and checked, one customer at a time.

Inside the document

A tree says where a document sits. Onboarding also has to read what is inside it: which paragraphs make a clause, which category each clause belongs to, and which tags it carries. The agent proposes all three. A person confirms them inside the document itself.

This is the interaction spec for that editor, running. At rest a paragraph shows nothing. Rest on it and its whole run gets a light box and a name. Click and it becomes a content control: edit the text, drag either boundary to take in the paragraph next door, tag a phrase, or select a run and split it out as its own clause with its own category. Three modes — browse, active, editing — and only one control is ever past browse at a time.

Dragging onto a paragraph another category already owns is refused: the border goes red and nothing is written. What should happen when the same category sits on both sides, whether a drag can be undone, and what two reviewers on one clause see are not decided here. The spec lists them as open questions for engineering instead of drawing an answer it does not have.

Decisions I'd defend

01

Review by company, not by file

The agent reads files. People think in companies. Grouping the review by the agent's normalised counterparty means one correction fixes every document in the group, and the screen shrinks to something a person can actually finish.

02

Reasoning instead of a score

The match panel says what the agent did: matched the names after ignoring company suffixes, here is the passage. A person can disagree with a sentence. Nobody can disagree with 0.82.

03

Proceed anyway, with the unmapped held back

Blocking family generation until every document is mapped would stall onboarding on the hardest few files. Instead the dialog states how many are unmapped, sends those back to the project queue, and generates families from what a person has confirmed.

04

Re-run is always on screen

Every review table has a visible Re-Run. Once a person has corrected a few mappings, the agent should be asked again with better inputs rather than the person finishing the rest by hand. Correction feeding the agent is the loop the whole design is built on.

What I can and cannot claim

The design shipped and customers are being onboarded through it. That much I can say.

What I cannot yet say is how much faster or more accurate onboarding has become. Internal material about this project carries figures, and I have chosen not to repeat them here because I do not know how they were measured or whether they were targets or results. A number I cannot explain is not evidence, it is decoration.

I am asking for post-launch usage data. When I have it and understand it, this section will change.

What I took from it

01

Put the checkpoint where the error compounds

One approval at the end feels efficient and is not. A wrong party name at step one is cheap to fix at step one and expensive to find at step three, buried in a hierarchy built on it.

02

Review the decision, not the input

The agent's unit of work was the document. The person's unit of judgment was the company. Designing the review around the second made it finishable.

03

The bulk action is the real interface

Most of the care in this design went into one button. Bulk Create is where the agent's guesses become the customer's permanent records, so it got a review step, a revert on every merge and a plain statement of what would happen.
