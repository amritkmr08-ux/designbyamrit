HOW I THINK / OWN WORK · 2023–26

When AI knows the answer, how does it know when to speak?

The same information can mean something very different depending on when it reaches you.

Your wife calls at 2 in the afternoon. You might answer or call back later.

But if she calls at 3 in the morning, you’d probably pick up immediately. Something might be wrong.

Same person. Same call. Yet you react differently because you understand the situation.

We make these judgments all the time. But can an AI agent do the same? When should it interrupt us, and when should it stay quiet?

01

Where it started

I was designing an AI-first contract tool. The agent reads agreements, connects clauses to amendments and invoices, and identifies things people might need to act on.

Imagine opening the tool in the morning to find 200 AI-generated insights.

You’d still have to read through everything to work out what matters. We’d simply replaced reading contracts with reading AI.

Showing only the top five seemed reasonable. But important isn’t always urgent. One finding can wait three months. Another might affect a payment going out in fifteen minutes.

The question wasn’t just what to show. It was when, to whom, and how loudly.

02

Same finding, different urgency

At 9 AM, the agent reads Acme’s payment term as Net 60. At 11 AM, an amendment arrives changing it to Net 45. Now consider two situations.

9:00Payment term: Net 60
11:00Amendment 3 arrives. Payment term: Net 45
Payment is three months awayQuietUpdate the term quietly. Keep the evidence. No need to interrupt anyone.
Finance is paying at noon using the old termInterruptShow the change before the payment goes out.

The finding hasn’t changed. What changed is how much attention it deserves.

Decision 1. Urgency depends on the situation, not just the finding.

03

What should the agent do?

This made me question whether changing the appearance of an alert was enough. Sometimes the agent should simply update information. Sometimes it needs to interrupt. And sometimes it should ask, or stop warning once the issue is handled.

I explored four responses to the same finding.

These aren’t just four ways of displaying an alert. They’re four different things the agent can do.

But there’s a catch. Asking also takes time. If a payment is going out in fifteen minutes, waiting for an answer may be worse than interrupting.

04

When the agent gets it wrong

Consider two contracts.

Contract A
Expires in two days. The vendor is already being offboarded.
Contract B
Expires in ten days. The renewal needs a decision this week.
By date A firstBy situation B first

Sort by date and Contract A comes first. But anyone who knows the situation would prioritise B. The agent could read every document correctly and still get the priority wrong.

I initially thought more context might solve this. But an agent can know 99 things about a contract and still miss the one fact that matters: Finance is paying at noon.

Knows 99 things

Missing: payment at noon

Stays quiet. Wrong call.

Knows 3 things

  • Payment at noon
  • Net 45 amendment
  • Finance owns it

Interrupts. Better-informed call.

Some facts may never reach the system. A renewal agreed over the phone. A supplier replaced after a meeting. An issue someone has already resolved.

The agent needs to be clear about what it knows and make it easy for people to correct it. “We’re already on it” isn’t just dismissing an alert. It’s giving the agent information it didn’t have.

Decision 2. More context doesn’t always mean better judgment. What matters is the fact that could change the decision.

05

What the agent needs to consider

These examples helped me narrow the problem to four questions.

FindingAn amendment changes the payment term
→
ConsequenceWhat happens if this is missed? Can it be undone?
TimeWhen does it start to matter?
Enough to judgeIs the information needed to make the decision missing?
PeopleWho owns the decision? Has someone already acted?
→
ResponseQuietSurfaceAskInterrupt

↺ When a person responds, that answer becomes part of the context for the next decision.

Together, these shape whether the agent stays quiet, shows something, asks, or interrupts.

Earlier, I’d explored five levels of attention, from Nothing to Interrupt. I still find them useful, but my thinking has shifted. The level of attention is the outcome. The harder problem is deciding which response the situation deserves.

06

What testing changed

I built a small clickable prototype around the Net 60 to Net 45 change. People saw three situations: a payment going out at noon, one in three weeks, and one where the payment date was unknown. For each, they chose whether the AI should interrupt, show a small sign, or ask a question.

Seven people tried it across two rounds, about two minutes each.

I expected the urgent payment to trigger an interruption, the distant one to stay quiet, and the unknown date to trigger a question. The first two went as expected. The third surprised me.

Nobody wanted the AI asking them when Finance would pay. They didn’t know. Finance did.

I’d been thinking about missing information without considering who could actually provide it. That changed the design.

What I learnedThe user may not know
→
AfterAsk Finance for me

Testing also revealed two other things

  1. Show the proof. People wanted to see the contract text before trusting an urgent warning.
  2. Let people return later. A quiet notification needed a “Remind me later” option.

Decision 3. Knowing who owns the decision matters as much as knowing how urgent it is.

This was a small test, and I didn’t record participants’ roles. These are early design directions, not validated rules.

But one thing stayed with me.

I had designed for missing information without asking who could supply it.

07

Where this stands

The idea that timing and context affect attention isn’t new. Horvitz explored interruption versus delay in Attention-Sensitive Alerting (1999). Dey examined context in 2001, and Microsoft’s Human-AI Interaction Guidelines (2019) address timing and correction.

What’s different now is that AI agents can find hundreds of things on their own, across documents and systems. But finding something doesn’t mean knowing what someone should do about it.

The agent may know a payment term changed. It may not know Finance already handled it. It may know a contract is expiring. It may not know the supplier is being replaced.

The agent can be right about what it found and still be wrong about what matters. That’s the part I’m interested in designing for.

How can an agent decide when something deserves our attention, especially when the most important part of the situation might be something it doesn’t know?
