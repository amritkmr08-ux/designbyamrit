An AI can extract 128 fields. A lawyer still has to check every one.

Three versions of the same screen, for checking what an AI pulled out of a contract. I built all three. This is what the first two got wrong, and what happened to retention once the third one shipped.

The task nobody can skip

Thousands of contracts. Hundreds of pages each. Someone has to read them closely enough to catch every risky clause, position, number and date. Miss one and it costs the company money, or leaves it exposed legally. So nobody skims. The work is slow because going fast is expensive.

An AI can read those pages in seconds and pull the values out. That part already worked. The model was not the problem.

The problem was the second half of the job. The machine proposes 128 answers. A lawyer has to agree with each one, and until they do, none of that speed counts. If checking the machine takes as long as reading the contract, the AI has moved the work, not removed it.

The extraction was fast. The verification was not. Everything I designed here lives in that gap.

What lawyers actually asked for

The instinct with an AI feature is to design for speed. Sitting with reviewers, speed turned out to be the wrong target. They didn't want the tool to tell them things faster. They wanted to see why it said them, without leaving the sentence they were on.

Three principles came out of that. Every decision below was settled against them.

Transparency over speed

A reviewer needs to see why a clause was flagged. An answer they can't trace back is an answer they have to redo themselves.

Human control at the centre

Assistance, never substitution. The machine proposes. The person accountable for the contract decides.

Continuity of flow

Break someone's focus and accuracy drops. Anything that pulls a reader out of the document has to earn the interruption.

Where the shape came from

Before designing anything I went looking for what reviewing already looks like when nobody has built a tool for it.

People review things all the time. Someone turns a packet over in a shop and runs a finger down the nutrition panel, checking the few numbers they care about. A rent agreement comes back from a tenant with highlighter through the clauses that matter. The marks sit on the sentences themselves, not in a summary somewhere else.

Both do the same thing, and it is worth copying: the mark stays on the source. Nobody copies the numbers into a separate sheet to check them. The mark goes where the evidence is, so the evidence and the judgment about it never come apart.

That gave the first version its shape, and it is the one rule that survived all three. What changed each time was how much could sit on the page at once without breaking the reading. What never changed: a reviewer should not have to leave the sentence to check the claim about it.

Three attempts at showing the machine's work

This is the honest sequence. Each version solved the complaint about the one before it and created a new one. I am including the two I replaced, because why each one failed is the actual finding.

01

Inline annotations

Highlight the extracted values right in the text, where the lawyer is already looking. No panel, no navigation, no second place to look. The most literal reading of "keep it in the document".

Worked

Fast to scan. The value stayed attached to the sentence that produced it.

Cost

Detail was hidden behind a hover. Reading stopped every time someone needed to check what a highlight meant.

Why I replaced it. Good for spotting things, too thin for deciding about them. Spotting is not verifying.
02

Hover cards

Open the highlight into a card that shows the extracted value next to the text, so a reviewer gets the context without losing their place. That fixed the depth problem directly.

Worked

More detail at a glance. Less guesswork, and checking a single value got quick.

Cost

Every check still needed a deliberate mouse move. Across a long document that is hundreds of small interruptions.

Why I replaced it. It solved one lookup well and a hundred lookups badly. The unit of work was never one value. It was a whole contract.
03

Assist bars

Stop hiding the labels. Put them in the margin, lined up with the passage each value came from, and leave them visible. Names, dates and clauses become a list a reviewer can work down, instead of things to go hunting for.

Worked

Ordered and structured. Review became one continuous pass instead of constant switching.

Cost

An extra layer of interface, and a real learning curve for people opening it for the first time.

Why this one stayed. It turned scattered highlights into a guided path through the document. It costs a new user something on day one and pays it back on every document after that.

The document moves, not the reviewer

The assist bars gave the review an order. What makes that order work is what happens after a single click.

Accept a value and three things happen, one after another instead of all at once. The tick lands on the value, because a person just decided it. A beat later the card closes and the next field opens. Then the contract scrolls itself to the passage that next value came from, with the passage lit.

That beat in the middle is doing real work. If the tick and the change of view landed at the same instant, the reviewer would lose sight of what they had just agreed to. So the tick gets its own moment before anything else moves.

What drops out of the loop is the navigation. It used to go: read the proposed value, scroll the document, find the clause, compare, scroll back, click. The reading and the deciding stay. The searching is gone, because the evidence for the next decision is already on screen when the reviewer gets there.

That is also the answer to the question this whole product turns on. You do not get a lawyer to trust a machine by telling them it is accurate. You put the source in front of them before they ask, on every value, without making them work for it.

Colour was not enough on its own

Four kinds of thing get extracted, and a reviewer has to tell them apart at a glance: metadata, clauses, obligations and service levels. The obvious answer is four colours. The obvious answer also leaves out colour-blind reviewers, and this is software people sit in for six hours a day.

So each type carries a shape as well as a colour. Circle, square, triangle, diamond. The difference holds up without colour, and costs nothing to anyone who can see it.

The number we decided not to show

Every AI feature hits the same fork. The model has some sense of how sure it is, so do you put that on screen? The default answer is a confidence score. We tried it, then took it out.

A lawyer cannot act on 0.82. It says the machine is fairly sure, which they already assumed, and it gives them nothing to check. Worse: a high number invites people to skip the check, and a low one tells them to look without telling them where. Either way the score stands in for the evidence instead of pointing at it.

What reviewers were actually doing was reading where the value came from. A party name pulled from the preamble was almost certainly right. One pulled from a recital halfway down page nine needed a second look. Where a value sits in a contract carries meaning, and lawyers already know how to read that. So the interface shows provenance instead of probability.

The card went through its own revision on the way here. An earlier version pasted in the surrounding sentence and gave a page number. Honest, but it left the reviewer to work out why that passage was the relevant one. Swapping the excerpt for the model's reasoning, and the page number for references you can jump to, kept the provenance and added the argument.

Two of those bands do the work a score would have done, and do it better.

The reasoning band makes the case in words. "Based on the last 12 documents, the standard term used for this document type is Master Service Agreement." That can be checked. A reviewer can disagree with the reasoning itself. You cannot disagree with a number.

The references band points at places in the contract. Select one and the document scrolls to the passage the value came from, so the reviewer is looking at the source, not at a claim about it. Verification becomes reading, which is the job lawyers were trained for, instead of trusting a number they have no way to audit.

What happens when it is wrong

Extraction gets things wrong. A review tool that only designs the agreeing path is not a review tool. Every proposed value has three ways out, not one.

Accept confirms it and moves to the next value, so agreement costs a single click and the reviewer keeps their place.

Edit fixes the value where it is. The field becomes editable in place, instead of opening a form somewhere else.

Re-tag is the one that matters. If the machine took the value from the wrong part of the document, the reviewer selects the right passage in the contract and the value is rebound to it. The correction is made against the evidence, not typed over the top of it, so the record keeps a source you can defend instead of an override nobody can explain.

Values come one at a time, not as a wall of 128. That was a deliberate trade. It costs a reviewer some sense of the whole, and buys an order they can work down without deciding what to look at next. On a hundred-page agreement, taking that decision away over and over is worth more than the overview.

Where a rejection goes

Rejecting a value takes it off the reviewer's screen, which is what they want. But it is also the most informative thing that happens all day, and throwing it away would be a waste. Every correction becomes a labelled example on the training side of the product, sorted by what kind of mistake it was: a value the model missed completely, a value it got wrong, or one a person tagged by hand.

That split matters. A miss and a wrong answer are different failures, and one "incorrect" bucket would hide which one the model is actually getting worse at.

Two decisions in that screen are the ones I would defend hardest.

A person chooses what the model learns from. Each example has a toggle and a validation state, so corrections get picked rather than swept in automatically. A reviewer having a bad afternoon should not be able to teach the model something wrong, and a duplicate should not count twice.

Training has a floor. The model cannot be retrained until there are at least twenty data points. A small rule, and it stops someone reacting to one bad extraction by retraining on it — which is exactly what people want to do when they are annoyed.

The loop only closes because the review screen captures why something was wrong, not just that it was. The two screens were designed as one system, and that is the part I would rather talk through than write down.

Then the field count grew

Assist bars worked. Then contracts turned up with a hundred and twenty-eight extracted fields, and the thing that had made review continuous started making it crowded. What reads as clear at forty fields reads as clutter at a hundred and twenty-eight.

Three questions had to be answered together. How does a reviewer switch between the four types? Where does filtering live? And how much can stay visible before visible turns into noise?

Option 1

Chips across the top

  • Extraction status readable at a glance, counts included
  • One click to move between categories
  • Visually cluttered once expanded
  • Fell apart past a hundred fields

Option 2

Grouped dropdown

  • Compact, and a familiar thing to reach for
  • Start broad, then go deeper into a category
  • Holds up as the dataset grows
  • More clicks than chips, and easy to miss if it sits too quietly

The dropdown won on scale, so I took it and fixed what it was bad at. Fields got grouped into real categories instead of one flat list, and the groups nest, so 128 fields reads as 64 performance review, then 34 obligations inside that. The filter stops being a list of everything and becomes a way to narrow down.

Months zero, one and two barely move. Slightly fewer people tried it, in fact. If the redesign had made the module more appealing, that is where it would show up. It doesn't.

The change is all at the back end of the curve. Before: month three was 14.8%, then month four fell off a cliff to 5%. People tried it, worked with it for a while, and went back to doing it by hand. After: month three holds at 22.3%, month four at 23.1%. The drop-off stops.

The redesign did not win more users. It stopped losing the ones who showed up. For a tool that has to be trusted before it is useful, that is the difference that matters.

What this data cannot tell you. The cohort nearly tripled between the two windows, from 860 users to 2,380, so it is not the same population, and some of the gain will belong to a different customer mix rather than the interface. The AI model was being improved over the same period too. Retention is a rate, not a count, which makes the comparison a fair one — but I would not claim the redesign is the only cause. What I do claim is narrower: the curve stopped decaying at the point the redesign landed, and the shape of that change, flat at the front and wide at the back, looks like a usability fix rather than a better model or a bigger sales year.

Alongside the numbers: the module went out in soft releases to customers including IBM and EY, and the response back was that it had become noticeably easier to work with. That is qualitative and I treat it that way — but it came from the accounts where being wrong costs the most.

What I took from it

01

Faster extraction is not faster review

The model was never the bottleneck. Nearly all of a reviewer's time goes on agreeing or disagreeing with the machine, and that is an interface problem, not a model problem. Teams building AI features keep investing in the half that already worked.

02

Design for the hundredth interaction

Hover cards tested beautifully on a single value and failed on a whole contract. Now I look at any interaction and ask what it costs when it happens a hundred times in an hour. That is the real usage, and it is not what a demo shows you.

03

Retention is the honest metric for trust

Efficiency percentages are easy to produce and easy to argue with. Whether people are still using the thing four months later is much harder to explain away. For AI features, where the real question is whether anyone believes the output, that is closer to what you wanted to know anyway.

04

Two of the three designs here are mine and failed

Inline annotations and hover cards were not someone else's bad ideas that I came in and fixed. I made them, watched them fall short, and replaced them. The sequence is the work. A case study that shows only the third version is describing a different job than the one I did.
