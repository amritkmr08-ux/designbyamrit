Sensitivity

A way for an agent to work out what a finding means for the person in front of it, decide how much attention that deserves, and then spend no more than that.

This paper takes one idea from an interaction-pattern page and works it through properly: where the idea came from, what already exists that looks like it, what is actually new in it, how it would work as a system, and where it breaks.

Opening scene

The same call, at 2 p.m. and at 3 a.m.

Your wife calls at two in the afternoon. You might answer, or call back later. She calls at three in the morning. You answer at once, half expecting something serious.

Nothing on the phone changed. Same name, same photo, same ring. What changed is everything around the call: the hour, what you expect of her, what you know about her week, what else could be going on. You did not add those up. You read them together, in an instant, and the call meant something different.

This is not a claim that people are predictable. Someone else, on another night, lets the 3 a.m. call ring out. The point is smaller: the meaning of a signal is not in the signal. It is in the situation the signal lands in, and that situation is a web of things that only make sense together. Three in the morning is not urgent on its own. It is urgent because of who is calling and what you know about them.

Agents are now in the position of the phone. They read contracts overnight, connect what they find across amendments and invoices, and reach conclusions nobody asked for. Each conclusion lands in someone's situation. The agent does not know that situation the way you know your wife. It can only see what has been written down. But it still has to decide: say something now, leave a note, or stay quiet.

Finding something and knowing what it means to this person today are two different problems. The second one is what this paper is about.The question

The rest of the paper takes that question into contract software, where the signals are payment terms and notice windows rather than phone calls. It asks what an agent would need to read a situation well enough to choose its response, and how that response should then appear on the page.

What just happened

In the feed, every conclusion got the same card. Nothing was wrong with any of them; the agent did good work. But the reader now has to open 200 cards to find the one where silence will cost them a quarter's figures. If each card takes 20 seconds to read and judge, that is over an hour before they reach it, assuming they reach it at all.

Sorted by Sensitivity, the same 200 conclusions become one thing that stops the reader, three that stand out, eight worth a line, twenty-four quiet notes, and 164 that say nothing because there is nothing to say. Not one conclusion was deleted. Each one was given the amount of attention that missing it would cost.

An agent's value is not how much it concludes. It is how few of its conclusions a person has to read.Why this paper exists

There is a catch in the second picture. "What missing it would cost" sounds like a number the agent can read off the contract. Often it cannot. The same conclusion costs a lot to miss for one reader this week and nothing for another, because of what is going on around it: who owns it, what is about to happen, what was decided somewhere the agent cannot see. Sorting is the visible half of the job. Reading the situation is the half that comes first.

Nobody asked. A search result lands in a question somebody typed. An agent's conclusion lands in nobody's question. It arrives at 3 a.m., unasked, and the agent has to judge for itself whether the person it concerns needs it today. That judgment used to be made by the person, in the act of looking. Now it has to be made before anyone looks.

The rest of the paper explains both halves: how the agent reads a situation, the five levels, the four questions that start the costing, where the explanation goes when the reader asks "why?", and where the whole idea breaks.

Before you start

How to read this paper

It is written so that someone who has never designed software can follow it. A few words are used in a specific way throughout. Here they are, once, so you never have to guess.

Agent

Software that does work on its own in the background, like reading all the contracts overnight and preparing a short list of things that need a person. In this paper, "the agent" is always that kind of software, never a human.

Moment

A point where the screen has something new to tell you. A deadline got closer. A value was read from a document. The agent finished a task. Every moment makes the interface choose how to respond.

Situation

Everything around a moment that changes what it means: the state of the work, who owns it, what is about to happen, what was decided somewhere else. The agent can only see the part of this that is written down.

Attention

The reader's noticing. It is limited and does not grow when the software gets smarter. Sensitivity treats attention as a budget.

Level

How much attention a moment is allowed to take, on a scale of five steps from Nothing to Interrupt. The reader never sees the word "level". They only feel that some things are easy to ignore and some are not.

Expression

Where and how the new information appears on screen. There are four kinds in this paper: Preview, Transform, Expand and Open. Each is explained with a working example you can click.

Source

Where a piece of information came from: read from a document, calculated from other facts, written by an AI model, or confirmed by a person.

Specimen

A small working example of one moment. The specimens in this paper are live: hover, click and drag them. Boxes marked live respond. Boxes marked static are pictures.

Three labels appear on claims. verified means I checked the source and it says what I say it says. proposal means this is my own idea and has not been tested with users. example data means the numbers exist only to make the example concrete.

Summary

The whole paper in one page

Software is getting very good at producing more. More answers, more recommendations, more explanations. People did not get more attention. So the design question changed.

The old question was how much can the screen show? The new one is how much attention does this moment deserve? Sometimes the answer is nothing. Sometimes a small hint. Sometimes a full stop, but only when missing it would cost something real.

Before deciding how the interface responds, decide how much attention the moment deserves. Then use the least interface that makes it clear.The rule

Sensitivity turns that rule into four decisions, made at every moment, in this order:

  1. What does this mean here? Before any level is chosen, the agent reads the finding against the situation it can see: the state of the contract, what is about to happen, who already owns it, what cannot be undone. The cost of missing is read from that, not from the finding alone, and the reading can be wrong when the situation is not written down.
  2. How much attention? Five levels: Nothing, Hint, Reveal, Emphasise, Interrupt. The level is set by what it would cost this person, in this situation, to miss the moment, not by how confident the software is.
  3. Where does the new thing go? Four expressions: Preview (appears beside, borrows a moment), Transform (replaces the old in place), Expand (sits next to the old), Open (takes over the screen). The choice depends on one test: do you need to see the old and the new at the same time?
  4. Where did it come from? Four sources: read from a document, calculated, generated by a model, or confirmed by a person. Each is shown differently, because each deserves a different amount of trust.

Parts of this exist already. Progressive disclosure, calm technology, phone notification levels and research on the cost of interruptions all overlap with it, and the paper names each one and says exactly what is borrowed. What Sensitivity adds is narrower than the concept page first claimed, and more defensible: one rule that covers both the reader asking for more and the system pushing more, applied inside the work itself, deciding where and when as well as whether, with the software's confidence kept separate from the cost of being wrong, and with the agent's reading of the situation shown on the page so a person can correct it.

The last part of the paper lists where the idea fails and what has not been tested. That part matters as much as the rest.

Part 1

Where the idea came from

Sensitivity did not start as a theory. It started as a problem on one screen: a contract page that was redesigned so that the software does the reading and the person only sees what needs them.

Part 1 · Origin

The contract page, before

A contract management system stores agreements between companies. Think of a master agreement with a supplier, plus the smaller documents that hang off it: statements of work, work orders, amendments. Each one has dates, money, obligations and deadlines inside it.

For years, the page for one contract looked like a filing cabinet: a tree of related documents on one side, typed-in fields on the other, and a stack of collapsed sections for the clauses. Everything was present, and everything was equally quiet. Nothing on it is wrong. It answers what is in the contract and leaves what should I do today entirely to the reader.

To find out whether anything needed doing, a person had to open each tab, read each row, remember the dates, and work out the consequences in their head. The software stored. The person thought.

Part 1 · Origin

The contract page, after

Before this page, the contract page was a filing cabinet: a header, forty fields, a list of documents, and all the reading left to the person. Nothing on it said what mattered. The redesign turned that around.

The redesign turned the page around. An agent reads every document in the family in the background. The page then opens with the few things that need a person today, written as decisions rather than data.

Read the three decision lines in the drawing. Each one is a complete thought: what to do, by when, and what it is worth. Behind each line, the agent did the reading that used to be the person's job. The page says less and means more.

That is where the trouble starts.

The trouble is the "why". Every line the agent writes is a conclusion, and the reader will want its reason before acting on it. The full paper shows what happens on the screen when they ask: the reason costs space, and space pushes the most dangerous item off the bottom. This story goes a different way, to the question the reasons raised. Of everything the agent could say, what does this person need today?

Part 1 · Origin

Four things pull against each other

Once readers start asking "why?", four forces meet on the same screen.

Force 1InformationEverything the agent knows and could show. It keeps growing as the agent does more.
Force 2SpaceThe pixels available. Roughly fixed. Screens have not grown anywhere near as fast as what agents can say.
Force 3TimeThe reader's moments. Limited, but the only budget that can be spent again and again in the same place.
Force 4ImportanceNot everything deserves the same share. A deadline that locks you in matters more than a field that was read correctly. And the share is not fixed to the item: the same deadline matters less once someone has decided to walk away.

Part 1 · Origin

Why this matters now, and not ten years ago

Screens have always been too small for everything. Designers have always had to choose. What is different about software built around agents?

Agents hand people conclusions. Old software handed people data and let them conclude. A field that says "Net 45" needs no explanation; you read it and you know. A line that says "counter at +6%" is a conclusion, and every conclusion owes an explanation. It arrives with a debt attached.

The debt is paid at a different time from the conclusion. The agent finished reading at 3 a.m. The reader checks the reasoning at 10 a.m., on their own schedule, one item at a time. The interface cannot know in advance which items will be checked, so it cannot pay all the debts up front without filling the screen.

Some conclusions are wrong. A model that reports 0.81 confidence should be wrong roughly one time in five, if that number is well calibrated. The reader must be able to find those cases. Hiding the reasoning saves space but makes the wrong cases invisible.

Not every conclusion matters equally. One line may be worth $134K and have three weeks to run. Another may be worth nothing in money but become binding in two days. If the interface treats them the same, the reader has to re-rank them in their head, which is the work the agent was supposed to remove.

Nobody asked. A search result lands in a question somebody typed. An agent's conclusion lands in nobody's question. It arrives at 3 a.m., unasked, and the agent has to judge for itself whether the person it concerns needs it today. That judgment used to be made by the person, in the act of looking. Now it has to be made before anyone looks.

So the question for a screen full of agent output is no longer "where do I put all this?" It is: for this moment, how much attention is right, and what is the cheapest way to spend it? Sensitivity is one answer to that question. The next part looks at the answers that already existed.

Part 2 · Prior work

Progressive disclosure

What it is. Show people the few most important things first, and offer the rest when they ask. The usability researcher Jakob Nielsen described it in a 2006 article: it "defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, making applications easier to learn and less error-prone." He adds that the idea is already more than thirty years old. verified

A close relative is a sentence from Ben Shneiderman's 1996 paper on visualisation, often called his mantra: overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand. verified

live · the classic form
Payment terms · Net 45
Read from clause §7.2. Extended from Net 30 by Schedule 2 on renewal. Confidence 0.82. Last verified 14 March 2026.
Progressive disclosure in its plainest form. Nothing shows until the reader asks. When they ask, it appears below.

What it does well

It keeps the first view clean and gives the reader control. Every "Show details" button, every "More" link, every expandable row is progressive disclosure, and it works.

Where it stops

  • It is the same every time. The designer decides once what goes on the surface and what goes behind the button. The split does not change when the situation changes. The "Net 45" row looks the same the day it was read and the day a dispute about it starts.
  • Only the reader pulls. It never pushes. If something becomes urgent while the details are hidden, progressive disclosure has no way to bring it forward.
  • It only knows "below". The details always appear as an addition. It does not ask whether the old line should stay, be replaced, or be compared with the new one.

Sensitivity uses progressive disclosure as one of its tools. The renewal example in Part 1 (headline, then short reason, then full reasoning) is progressive disclosure. What Sensitivity adds is the rule for how far to go and when to go there without being asked.

Part 2 · Prior work

Calm technology

What it is. In 1996, Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown at Xerox PARC wrote a short paper called "The Coming Age of Calm Technology". Its idea: technology should live mostly at the periphery of our attention, "what we are attuned to without attending to explicitly", and move to the centre only when it matters. "A calm technology will move easily from the periphery of our attention, to the center, and back." verified

Their best-known example is the Dangling String, an installation by the artist Natalie Jeremijenko: an eight-foot plastic tube hung from a motor wired to the office network. Heavy network traffic made the string twitch and whir; quiet periods barely moved it. Nobody read a screen. People sensed the network from the corner of their eye. verified

In 2015, Amber Case turned this into a set of principles in her book Calm Technology. The first is: "Technology should require the smallest possible amount of attention." Another: "The right amount of technology is the minimum needed to solve the problem." verified

This is the closest existing idea to Sensitivity. The sentence "use the least interface that makes it clear" is a restatement of Case's first principle. The paper does not claim that sentence as new.

Where it stops

  • Two zones, not a scale. Calm technology has the periphery and the centre. It does not say how many steps lie between them, or what each step looks like on a screen.
  • No rule for the move. It says information should move to the centre "when it matters" but does not say how software decides that it matters.
  • It was written for rooms, not screens. The examples are strings, lights and windows. The question of whether a new fact replaces an old one, or sits beside it, does not come up.

The fairest description of Sensitivity in these terms: calm technology made usable inside a working screen, with named steps, a test for when to climb them, and specific ways to place the new thing.

Interruption levels, and the cost of interrupting

Two more pieces of existing work come even closer to the "how much attention" question.

Apple's notification interruption levels

Since iOS 15 (announced at WWDC 2021), an app sending a notification chooses one of four levels. verified

This is a ladder of how hard something may interrupt you, four steps against Sensitivity's five. The difference is scope. Apple's levels apply to messages arriving from outside the app you are using. They say nothing about a value inside a document, a row in a list, or a button you are about to press. They also control only loudness, not placement.

The cost of interrupting, as a calculation

In 1999, Eric Horvitz, Andy Jacobs and David Hovel at Microsoft Research published "Attention-Sensitive Alerting". Their system, called Priorities, looked at incoming email and decided whether to alert the user now or later. The abstract describes procedures that "balance the context-sensitive costs of deferring alerts with the cost of interruption." verified

Read that sentence slowly. The cost of deferring is what you lose by seeing something later rather than now. That is almost exactly what this paper calls the cost of missing. The idea of weighing it against the cost of being interrupted is more than twenty-five years old.

Where both stop

  • Both decide whether and how loudly to interrupt. Neither decides where the information goes or whether it replaces something.
  • Both work at the edge of the application (a notification arriving). Sensitivity works inside it (a fact in a row).
  • In Horvitz's framing, as far as I can tell from the abstract, the likelihood that a message matters and the cost of missing it are combined into one expected cost. Sensitivity keeps the software's confidence and the cost of missing apart, for a reason explained in Part 3. I have not read the full paper closely enough to be certain they never separate the two, so treat this difference as likely rather than proven.
Part 2 · Prior work

What the evidence says about asking for too much attention

Sensitivity's rule is "spend no more attention than the moment deserves". Is overspending actually harmful, or just untidy? Three studies say it is harmful, in measurable ways.

Repeated alerts get ignored

Ancker and colleagues (2017) studied clinical alerts in a hospital's electronic records. They found that "likelihood of reminder acceptance dropped by 30% for each additional reminder received per encounter". Roughly a quarter of drug alerts and a third of clinical reminders were duplicates for the same patient within a year. verified

In plain terms: every extra alert made the next one less likely to be acted on. Doctors were not careless. The system had trained them to click past it. This is the cost of a level set too high too often.

Interruptions make people faster and more stressed

Gloria Mark, Daniela Gudith and Ulrich Klocke (2008) measured people doing an email task with and without interruptions. The interrupted groups finished slightly faster (about 20 minutes against about 23) but reported higher stress (9.46 against 6.92 on their scale), higher frustration, more time pressure and more effort. People compensate for interruptions by working faster, and pay for it. verified

People can hold very little at once

Nelson Cowan (2010) summarised decades of memory research: working memory holds about "3 to 5 meaningful items in young adults". verified A screen that asks the reader to remember a number from one panel to understand another is spending one of those three to five slots. This is why the Expand expression in Part 3 exists: when two facts must be compared, keep both on screen so the reader does not have to carry one.

What this does and does not prove. These studies support the claim that over-alerting and interrupting are costly. None of them tested Sensitivity itself. The specific levels and rules in Part 3 remain a proposal.

Part 3 · Before the axes

Before attention comes judgment

The three axes that follow (how much attention, where it goes, where it came from) all start from one input: what missing this moment would cost. This page is about where that input comes from. It does not come from the finding. It comes from reading the finding against the situation.

An agent that reads a contract correctly has done the first job. It knows the payment term is Net 45, that it was Net 60 until an amendment last month, and which page says so. The second job is to work out what that means for the person who will see it, today. These are different jobs, and a system can be good at the first and bad at the second.

What can an agent read about a situation? Only what is recorded, but that is more than the contract. Seven kinds of thing, offered as candidates and not as a score. proposal

The finding and its evidence. Net 45, from Amendment 2, section 3. The state of the work. Is this contract in renewal, in dispute, being wound down, or simply running? What is about to happen. A payment run, a signature, a notice window closing, recorded in a calendar, a workflow or a task someone opened. What missing or delaying it would cost. Money, a lost right, a deemed acceptance. Who already owns it. A task assigned, a reply drafted, a colleague who opened the clause yesterday. Whether it can be undone. A draft can; a sent notice cannot. What is not available. The gaps the agent knows it has.

None of these means anything alone. That is the lesson of the 3 a.m. call, and it is why this is not a checklist to be ticked and summed. A payment run next week is a fact. Net 45 is a fact. Put together, they say an invoice may go out on the wrong term. Add that someone in finance corrected the run yesterday, and the same two facts say nothing. Add instead that the run is for $412,000, and they say a great deal. The judgment lives in the relationships between the inputs, not in any one of them, and the relationships move as the situation does.

Knowing that something is true is not the same as knowing whether anyone needs it now. The first is read from the documents. The second is read from the situation the documents sit in.The split

The output of this judgment is not a yes or a no. It is a reading: this change matters to this person because of that payment, and it matters today. The level, the expression and the wording all follow from that reading. That is why the rest of Part 3 can treat "the cost of missing" as a given: it is a given once the situation has been read. The reading can be wrong, and the page on confidence later in this partscreen 8 says what to do about that. First, the same finding read twice.

Judging whether and when to act is not new. Context-aware computing and mixed-initiative interaction have worked on it since the 1990s, and the full paper lines each older idea up against this one and says exactly what is borrowed.

Part 3 · Before the axes

The same finding, read in two situations

A worked example, invented. example data At 9 a.m. the agent's reading of a supplier contract says the payment term is Net 60. At 11 a.m. an amendment that was signed last month is finally uploaded. The agent reads it: the term is Net 45, from the first of this month. Same contract, same agent, better evidence. What should it do with that?

Situation A · nothing is about to happen

The next invoice on this contract is due in three months. Nobody has the contract open. No task mentions it.

The agent updates the term, keeps the Net 60 reading and the amendment side by side in the history, and marks the change for whoever opens the contract next. Level: Nothing now, a Hint when someone next looks. An interruption here would be noise with a good excuse.

Situation B · a payment run at noon

Finance has a payment run scheduled for 12:00 today, and it was built on Net 60. The run is in a system the agent can read.

Now the same finding sits one hour from a wrong payment. The agent brings it to the person who owns the run, with the amendment attached: Interrupt, carried outside the page if they are not in it. After noon it drops back to a record of what was found and who was told.

The finding did not change between the two columns. Its evidence did not change. What changed is what sits next to it: an action, a time, an owner, and the money in play. The relationships between those moved the level from Nothing to Interrupt. Take any one away and it moves back.

The dependency, stated plainly. Situation B only works if the noon payment run is recorded somewhere the agent can read. If finance planned it in a meeting and nobody wrote it down, the agent is in Situation A, and it will be wrong. Nothing in this paper assumes the agent knows private plans. And not every change to a payment term touches a payment: the run may already be on the right term, or on a different contract. The agent has to check that too, from what it can see.

Part 4 shows an item moving as a deadline nears, day by day. This page is the other kind of movement. Nothing climbed here. New evidence arrived at 11, and a scheduled action at 12 gave it its weight.

Part 3 · The model

Attention is the budget. Space and time are how it gets spent.

Think of the reader's attention as money. The interface spends it every time it shows something. There are two currencies.

Space
How much is on screen at once. Spending space means adding: another line, another panel, another column. Space is paid by the page. It runs out, and when it does, things fall off the bottom.
Time
The same place on screen, used again at a later moment. Spending time means replacing: the line becomes its explanation, then becomes the clause. Time is paid by the reader, from memory. It never runs out, but it costs remembering.

Most interfaces only know how to spend space. The "why" panel in Part 1 spent space and pushed the deadline off the screen. A tooltip spends a little space for a little time. A full-screen dialog spends all the space and all the time at once.

Choose the least expensive interaction that gives the moment enough attention.The spending rule

Two things follow from treating attention as a budget.

  • Nothing is a valid amount. Most moments deserve no spend at all. A contract that is fine should say nothing. Silence is the signal that nothing is wrong.
  • Time can stand in for space, but only sometimes. If the old information has finished its job, replace it and save the pixels. If the reader needs to compare old and new, replacing them forces the reader to hold one in memory, and Cowan's three-to-five-item limit from Part 2 makes that expensive. Then space is the cheaper currency.

The rest of this part is the budgeting method: how much to spend (the level), which currency to spend it in (the expression), and how much the reader should trust what they bought (the source).

Confidence is not consequence

Two different numbers are easy to confuse. Confidence is how sure the software is that it read or reasoned correctly. Consequence is how much it would cost to miss or get wrong. Most systems blend them: lower confidence means more warnings. Sensitivity keeps them apart, and gives each a different job.

  • Consequence sets the level. How much attention the moment may take.
  • Confidence sets the wording and the permission. Whether the system says "is" or "appears to be", whether it shows its basis, and whether it is allowed to act alone or must ask first.

Why keep them apart? Because they fail in opposite ways. If low confidence raised the level, a system that is unsure about many small things would shout constantly, and the reader would learn to ignore it. If high confidence lowered the level, a system that is sure about a $2M liability cap would whisper it. Being sure and being important are different questions, and so are being sure and being allowed to act.

Sure about the clause, unsure about the situation

There is a second kind of confidence hiding inside the first, and it is worth separating without turning it into a third dial. The slider above measures confidence in the finding: did the agent read Net 45 correctly? There is also confidence in the reading of the situation: given everything the agent can see, is this the moment that finding matters, and to this person? An agent can be at 0.98 on the first and quietly wrong on the second, because the second depends on what it cannot see.

The two renewals on the previous pagefrom screen 6 are the plain case. The agent read both notice windows perfectly. It did not know that Supplier A was being let go, if that decision had only been spoken and never recorded. Its finding was right. Its judgment about who needed it was wrong, and no amount of re-reading the contract would fix that.

What follows for the design is modest. The agent shows what its reading rested on, in a line, not a dashboard: "Raised because a payment run is scheduled for 12:00." Where the reading leans on an assumption, the wording says so, the way the slider above turns "is" into "appears to be". And the person can correct the reading in one step: "Already handled", "We are leaving this supplier", "Not my contract". The correction changes the record and the next judgment on this item. It does not retrain a model, and the design should not promise that it does.

Three ways this fails. When the context was never written down: a decision made in a hallway never reaches the agent, so it raises an item that is already dead and stays quiet about one that is alive. When the agent guesses what you are doing: you opened the contract, so you must be about to act. That inference is often wrong, and an Interrupt built on it is an ambush. And when it is right on the data and wrong for the person: the person already knows, has decided, or is the wrong person. Being right about the contract does not make an interruption right.

Four ways to place the new thing

The second question is where does the new information go, relative to what is already there? There are four answers. Two spend time, two spend space.

spends time · borrowsPreviewNew information appears around the existing object for a moment. The original keeps its place and stays active. Close it and nothing needs restoring.
spends time · replacesTransformThe existing thing becomes its own next state, in the same slot. The previous state is given up. A tab switches between equals; a Transform replaces a thing with its own explanation.
spends space · besideExpandNew information sits next to the original, in the same local context. Both can be read at once.
spends space · overOpenA new context takes over, with its own focus, scroll and exit. The original waits underneath.

The test for choosing between time and space is one question:

Can the reader meaningfully look at the old and the new information at the same time? If yes, spend space (Expand or Open). If one takes over the other's reading moment, spend time (Preview or Transform).The placement test

Two things to be clear about.

  • This is about what the reader perceives, not how it is built. A panel that is positioned absolutely and does not reflow the page is still an Expand if the reader can read both. A panel that covers the original is a Preview even though technically it "adds" an element. The classification is perceptual.
  • Expression is not a ladder. Preview is not "low" and Open is not "high". A level-Interrupt moment can be a Transform (the line becomes a question). A level-Hint moment can be an Open (a quiet link that opens the clause). Level says how much; expression says where.

Part 4 · Time

When the reader is not there

Everything in this part has a hidden assumption: that the reader opens the product. An Interrupt that waits on a page nobody visits interrupts nobody. If the reviewer is on leave for the week of 22 May, the climb happens in an empty room and silence still counts as acceptance.

So at the top of the ladder, Sensitivity has to step outside the screen. This is the one place where it meets the notification systems from Part 2 directly.

Three rules for stepping outside

  1. Only Interrupt goes outside. Hint, Reveal and Emphasise live on the page. If the item never reaches Interrupt, the reader never gets an email about it. This is what keeps the inbox quiet enough that the one email that does arrive is read.
  2. Outside carries the same reason. The email says "2 days left. Silence means you accept the Q2 review." It does not say "You have a new notification." The reason is what earned the interruption; it must travel with it.
  3. Outside has a fallback person. If the reader is marked away, the Interrupt goes to whoever covers for them, and the page shows that it did. An Interrupt that reaches nobody has failed, and the system should know it failed.

What changed

  • The usual dialog asks a yes/no question with no basis. The reader confirms from habit, or cancels from doubt. Neither is a decision.
  • The Sensitivity line is longer on the surface (the count above shows it) but it carries the consequence ($134K), the one-line reason, and the alternative. The full reasoning is a Preview away and costs nothing until asked.
  • "Confirm" became "Draft the counter". The button says what will actually happen next, and what happens next is reversible: a draft, not a sent offer.
  • The situation the agent assumed, and should say: the window is open until 1 June, nobody has countered yet, and the seat data is current. If procurement had already decided to drop this supplier, the right level is Nothing, and the agent only knows that if the decision is recorded.

Where it would break

If the reader has never seen this kind of recommendation before, a Preview is too quiet for the first time. They do not know the line carries a reason one step away. The first encounter probably deserves an Expand with the reason open, and later encounters the Preview. Sensitivity as written does not yet say how the interface should know it is the first time.

Part 5 · Specimen 2

Send the summary to 42 customers

The moment. The agent has drafted a weekly summary from the team's activity. It will go to 42 customers. Cost of missing: a wrong summary reaches 42 people and cannot be recalled after 4:00 PM. Level: Emphasise, at the moment of sending only.

What changed

  • The usual pattern offers "Undo" after sending. That is a promise the system often cannot keep: once the email is in 42 inboxes, Undo recalls nothing. Sensitivity names the real point of no return (4:00 PM) before the button, not a fake one after it.
  • The one line in red is the only thing at level Emphasise. Everything else on the card is at Reveal. The reader's eye goes to the one fact that should make them pause.
  • The numbers (12 shipped, 3 moved, 1 blocked) are the summary of the summary. The reader can check the shape of what is being sent without opening it. If the shape is wrong (12 shipped when they know it was 9), they stop.
  • The situation the agent assumed: the send is scheduled for 4:00 PM, the list of 42 was built from the current customer set, and nobody has reviewed it yet. If a colleague had already checked the draft this morning, the red line should soften to Reveal. The card should be able to say which of those it checked.

Where it would break

If this card appears every week with the same red line, the red line stops being red in the reader's mind. The Ancker finding from Part 2 applies: repetition cuts response. A better design might show the point-of-no-return line only when something is different from last week (more recipients, an attachment, a blocked item), and stay at Reveal when the send is routine. That is a judgment Sensitivity's current rules do not make.

Part 5 · Specimen 3

Sign or annotate the Q2 review by 22 May

The moment. A quarterly review has been sent for sign-off. The statement of work says that no response by 22 May counts as acceptance. Cost of missing: the figures become final without anyone having agreed to them. Level: climbs from Hint to Interrupt as the date approaches (Part 4).

Usual

Q2 Review
Due 22 May · Status: Pending

Sensitivity, at 2 days left

Sign or annotate the Q2 review
2 days left. No reply by 22 May counts as acceptance under SOW Section 8.
One short note is enough to keep the question open.

What changed

  • "Due 22 May · Pending" is true and useless. It does not say what happens on 23 May. The Sensitivity version says the consequence in the reader's own terms: silence is acceptance.
  • The cheapest safe action is named: "one short note is enough". Without that line, a busy reader might assume they need to review everything and put it off, which is exactly the outcome the clause punishes.
  • All three choices are visible, including the one the system does not recommend (Accept). An Interrupt that only offers the "right" answer is not asking, it is nagging.
  • The situation the agent assumed: this reader is the one who signs, nobody has replied, and the date has not been extended. Each of those is a recorded fact the Interrupt rests on, and the line "2 days left" is the only one of them the card currently shows.

Where it would break

If the reader is not the right person (the review should be signed by their manager), the Interrupt lands on someone who cannot act, and Sensitivity has no rule for handing it on. The "fallback person" idea from Part 4 covers absence, not wrong recipient. That gap is real and unsolved here.

A model that only shows its successes is a sales deck. This part lists the places Sensitivity breaks, what has not been tested, and the honest size of the claim.

Part 6 · Limits

Where Sensitivity fails

1. When hiding a relationship hides the point

The notice-period case in Part 3 is the warning. A Transform that shows one amendment at a time looked elegant and hid the one thing worth seeing: the climb from 30 to 90 days. Any time the question is "how did we get here?", the least interface is the wrong interface. The comparison test exists to catch this, but it depends on the designer asking it.

2. When the law says show it anyway

Some information must be shown regardless of what it costs in attention: a regulatory disclosure, a term the other party must be able to see, a warning a court would expect. Sensitivity's "Nothing" level is not available for those. The model needs a floor that the cost-of-missing rule cannot lower, set by someone who knows the law, not by the design.

3. When generated text looks retrieved

The source marks in Part 3 are small by design. That is also their weakness. If a reader stops noticing the difference between the teal dot and the violet one, a model's guess reads with the authority of a signed clause. This is the most dangerous failure in the whole model, because it fails silently and in the direction of over-trust. Wording ("appears to be", "the agent suggests") has to carry the difference as well as the mark, and even then it may not be enough.

4. When everything climbs

Every rule in Part 3 that raises the level has to be matched by a rule that keeps things down, or the system drifts upward until it is a wall of Emphasise and the reader tunes out. This paper names that risk but does not solve it. A real system would need a budget per screen, something like "at most one Interrupt and two Emphasise at a time", and a way to decide which items lose when the budget is exceeded.

5. Without a mouse

Preview is the cheapest expression and the one most tied to hover. On a phone, every Preview needs a visible trigger and a tap. For a screen reader, a Preview that appears visually but is not announced is simply absent. Each specimen in this paper has a keyboard path, but that was added one by one; the model has no general rule for it yet.

6. When the reader is new

The whole model assumes the reader has learned which quiet cues carry a reason. A first-time reader has not. Sensitivity may need a louder first week and a quieter second one, and nothing here says how to do that.

7. When the context was never written down

The judgment in Part 3 reads only what is recorded. A decision made in a hallway, a supplier being replaced after a call, a colleague already on it: none of these reach the agent. It will raise an item that is already dead and stay quiet about one that is alive. The one-step correction is the only defence, and it only works if correcting is cheaper than ignoring.

8. When the agent guesses what you are doing

Situation B in Part 3 works because the payment run is in a system. The tempting next step is to infer activity: you opened the contract, so you must be about to act. That inference is often wrong, and an Interrupt built on it is an ambush. The model should raise the level on recorded actions and treat inferred ones as a Hint at most. Nothing here says where that line sits.

9. When it is right on the data and wrong for the person

Everything in the four questions can be true and the interruption still unwelcome: the person already knows, has decided, or is the wrong person. Being right about the contract does not make an interruption right. This is the failure the 3 a.m. call warns about, and the one a first test should look for hardest.

What a first test would look like

Take the three deep specimens from Part 5in the full paper (counter the renewal, send to 42 customers, sign or annotate by 22 May). Show six contract reviewers the usual version and six the Sensitivity version. Measure three things: how long until they act, whether they act correctly (counter, review recipients, reply), and whether they can say afterwards why the system recommended what it did. The third measure is the one that matters most, because it tests whether the "why" debt from Part 1 was actually paid.

A fourth measure, for the judgment pages in Part 3: wherever the agent's proposed level and a reviewer's own call disagree, write down the fact that explains the gap. Those facts are the real list of situation inputs. They will be more useful than the seven guessed here, and some of them will turn out to be things no system can be told.

Part 6 · The claim

The honest size of the claim

The concept page this paper started from said, in effect, "here is a new way to think about interfaces". After Part 2, that is too big. Here is what can actually be defended.

Part 2 already lined up each existing idea against what Sensitivity adds. Stripped of the comparison, the claim is seven specific decisions:

  1. The reading of the situation carried through to the screen. What is about to happen, who owns it and what the agent cannot see set the level, show in the wording, and can be corrected in one step. Judging whether and when to act is older work (Horvitz, Dey, Part 2). Connecting that judgment to placement, wording and correction inside the work is the part claimed here.
  2. Five named steps between periphery and centre, each defined by what the reader experiences.
  3. One rule for pulling and pushing, so a Hint can become an Interrupt without anyone clicking.
  4. The ladder inside the page, applied to a value, a row or a button, not only to a notification.
  5. Confidence kept apart from the cost of missing, each controlling a different thing: wording and permission versus level. Likely new, not certain. Horvitz's 1999 attention-sensitive alerting was checked from its abstract only; his mixed-initiative principles from the same year were read in full, and they weigh attention in the timing of a service, not in its wording or permission.
  6. Transform as a thing becoming its own explanation, with no control and no siblings, and a stated rule for when to use Expand instead.
  7. The climb paired with ways back down, and a rule for stepping outside the screen only at the top.

That list is the claim. It is a set of specific, checkable design decisions, not a philosophy. Each one could turn out to be wrong in testing, and each one is small enough that being wrong about it would not sink the others.

What is not claimed: a new way for software to judge situations. Context-aware computing and mixed-initiative interaction have worked on that since the 1990s, and the seven inputs in Part 3 are a designer's list of what an agent would need to read, not an algorithm for reading it. The claim is only that the judgment should be visible, placed, worded and correctable on the page where the work happens.

On the question of patents

Principles cannot be patented. Specific mechanisms sometimes can. Of the seven, the items most like mechanisms are the Transform-to-Expand switch triggered by back-and-forth reading (Part 3, flagged as untested), the two-input rendering where confidence and consequence control different outputs (Part 3), and the climb-with-descent rules (Part 4). I am not a patent attorney and have not run a search. Two cautions before anyone does: most of the examples here are contracts, so an employer may have a claim on contract-specific inventions, and the closest existing work (Horvitz et al.) is from a company with a large patent portfolio in exactly this area.

Part 6 · Reach

The same rule outside contracts

Contracts are a good place to find this problem because the cost of missing is often written in the document. But the rule does not depend on contracts. Three other places where an agent hands people conclusions, and what the three axes would do there.

Code review

An agent reviews a change and leaves twenty comments. Most are style. Two are a bug and a security hole. Level: style comments at Hint (a dot in the margin), the bug at Reveal, the security hole at Emphasise. Expression: the comment Transforms into the failing case on click; the security hole Expands to show the line and the exploit side by side, because the reader must compare them. Source: "the linter found" (retrieved, from a rule) is marked differently from "the model thinks" (generated).

Email

An assistant drafts replies. Level: a reply to a newsletter is Nothing (sent, logged). A reply that commits money or a date is Reveal before sending. A reply to a legal thread is Emphasise. Expression: the draft Previews under the original message so the reader can check it against what was asked. Source: any figure the model inserted that was not in the thread is marked generated, so the reader checks it before it goes out. This is the "generated looks retrieved" failure from the limits page, in the place it does the most harm.

Hospital alerts

This is where the evidence in Part 2 came from, and where the cost of getting the level wrong is highest in both directions. An alert at Interrupt for every drug interaction trains clinicians to click through (Ancker's 30% per repeat). The Sensitivity reading: level from the severity of the interaction and whether the order can be changed later, not from the confidence of the match; expression Transform for the common case (the order line becomes the warning in place) and Interrupt only for the rare severe one; source a rule-based match (retrieved) marked differently from a model's inference (generated). I would not design this without clinicians, and the paper does not claim the model is ready for it. It claims the questions are the right ones to ask.

In all three, the specific levels and thresholds would be different. The three questions would not.

Closing

The moment decides the attention. The interface decides how gracefully to spend it.

Software that does the work for people owes them two things: the conclusion, and a cheap way to check it. Sensitivity is a method for paying the second debt without bankrupting the first.

Four questions, asked at every moment: what does this mean for this person now, how much attention does that deserve, where should it go, and where did it come from. A ladder with five steps and ways down as well as up. A placement test with one question. Four source marks. Most of the time, the right answer is nothing at all.

None of it is finished. The limits page is longer than the claims page, and that is the correct ratio for an idea at this stage.
