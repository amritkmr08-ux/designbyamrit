Designing human-guided AI for contract onboarding.

Legal teams don't lack data — they struggle to trust it. At scale, small onboarding errors compound into broken relationships and unreliable systems. So the design question isn't how AI replaces the humans; it's where their judgment actually belongs.

Humans in the loop, agents in the flow

Agents do the reconstruction. People answer three questions, in order:

01

Identify

"Who is this?" Resolving legal entity names from fuzzy strings into a single source of truth.

02

Validate

"What's connected to it?" Mapping parent–child hierarchies between agreements automatically — MSAs, SOWs, amendments, rate cards — so relationships are explicit, not inferred.

03

Verify

"Can I trust this?" Presenting AI evidence to humans for final, high-confidence approval.

Design principles that made trust possible

A

Agents surface ambiguity instead of hiding it

Low-confidence extractions are flagged with distinct visual markers, prompting human audit exactly where it's needed — the system never pretends to be more certain than it is.

B

Reasoning is one click away

Every data point links directly back to the original PDF clause for immediate verification. Trust isn't asked for — it's inspectable.

C

Confidence comes from traceability

Data lineage is preserved at every step, creating a permanent audit trail from source document to system of record.

The hardest call: when should the agent act, and when should it ask?

Too eager, and the agent silently writes errors into a legal record. Too cautious, and you've rebuilt the manual queue with extra steps. The rule set turns on two questions: how confident is the agent, and how costly is being wrong?

Act

Confident + reversible

The agent resolves it and moves on. The action lands in the work log — visible if you look, silent if you don't.

Act, then flag

Confident + consequential

The agent acts but surfaces the decision for spot-checking, with evidence one click away.

Flag for audit

Uncertain + reversible

Low-confidence extractions get distinct visual markers and queue for batch review — they don't block the pipeline.

Ask

Uncertain + consequential

The agent stops and asks one clear question — like the entity match above — with its suggestion and reasoning attached.

Axes: agent confidence × cost of being wrong. Asking is the exception, not the default — that's what keeps humans in the loop without putting them back on the assembly line.

One distinction does most of the work, and I later wrote it into the platform's design direction: confidence is not permission. Certainty belongs to the model; authority belongs to the relationship. So the checkpoint sits at the irreversible step, and only there.

Designing the states nobody demos

A pipeline that only works when everything goes right isn't a pipeline. Most of the design time went here — and this is where trust is actually won or lost.

Working

Live counters name the document being processed ("07_WO1_under_SOW1.pdf"), not a spinner. Progress you can read is progress you believe.

Low confidence

Distinct visual marking, never a silent pass. Flagged fields queue for batch review rather than blocking the pipeline.

Partial success

The common case. A batch can be 84% clean and still useful — the ready set activates while the rest stays held.

Held

A first-class state, not an error. Held items stay searchable and counted; every headline number reconciles live as decisions land.

Missing dependency

An amendment whose parent isn't in the batch is marked awaiting-parent and auto-links later when the parent arrives — no rework.

Skipped

Skipping review is allowed, but recorded as a caveat that travels with the data downstream. Convenience never erases the record.

Inherited

Values pulled from a parent agreement are labelled inherited, not presented as if they were read from the document.

Undo & audit

Every accepted value keeps its lineage — source clause, extraction version, approver — so any decision can be traced and reversed.

Disagreement

Users can reject the agent's framing outright with "this question is wrong". A system that can't be contradicted can't be trusted.

Bulk action

Where one cause holds many contracts, the verb is plural — merge, map, inherit all — so clearing a pattern is one decision, not fifty.

What I rejected

A confidence percentage on every field

Showing "87%" beside each value felt transparent and tested badly — people either ignored the numbers or over-trusted them, and nobody could say what 87% meant for their decision. Replaced with three states tied to what the user should do: accept, check, decide.

A single review queue

The obvious model — one list of everything needing attention. It reproduced the manual process it was meant to replace and got longer the better the AI got at finding problems. Replaced with pattern-first triage: resolve the cause, clear every contract under it.

Blocking activation until the portfolio was clean

Intuitively correct, operationally fatal — a handful of ambiguous documents would hold an entire portfolio hostage for weeks. Replaced with partial activation: ready contracts go live, held ones stay visible and keep working in the background.

What I learned

The hardest part wasn't the AI. It was deciding when the system should explain, recommend, act, or disappear. Early versions over-showed their work, and users felt like they were watching software perform rather than delegating to an operator. The fix: show reasoning while trust is being earned, then collapse to exceptions once it is.
