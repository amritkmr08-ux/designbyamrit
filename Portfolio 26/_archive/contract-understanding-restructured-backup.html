Case study — System design

One question. Our system couldn't answer it.

"Which amendment applies to this SOW?" I redesigned the repository from a folder hierarchy into a relationship model that makes governing agreements, amendments and execution agreements explicit — for the people reading them, and for the AI reasoning over them.

Problem

Reviewers spent 15–45 minutes per contract reconstructing which amendments applied.

Insight

Legal teams reason about what governs what. The system only stored where files sat.

Decision

Rebuild agreements as entities with typed relationships, rather than improve the folder browser.

Status

Recently shipped. Internal target: ~40% less review effort. Not yet measured.

Users had already built the model — outside the product

The folder tree isn't wrong. It answers "where is this file?" faithfully. But the question people actually ask is "what governs this contract?" — and when a system doesn't answer that, users answer it themselves, with whatever they have.

MSA_v3_FINAL_governs_SOW-Boston_supersedes_Amend2_EXECUTED.pdf

A governing relationship, an applicability, and a supersession — all encoded in a file name, because the system had no field for any of them.

The structure lived in one person's memory

At one enterprise customer, a legal ops manager left — and her knowledge of which amendment applied to which SOW left with her. Her replacement needed weeks of handover.

The relationship map lived in Confluence

Pages titled "Contract Family Structure." Spreadsheets of parent–child relationships. The model users needed already existed — maintained by hand, alongside the product, going stale.

"Contracts are understood through what governs what — not through files."

Customer success director

"Amendments are often filed under SOWs, but they actually impact the MSA. That mismatch creates confusion."

VP, standards and processes
Drawn from three logged product escalations, a customer feedback review and a working session with an enterprise legal team — ten separately-reported problems that collapsed into one insight: the system had to represent legal relationships, not folders.

The decision I pushed for

It presented as a search problem, and we tried what search offers — filters, OCR, indexing. None of it moved the needle, because users weren't searching. They were reconstructing meaning.

Option A

Optimise the folder browser

Faster, safer, shippable in weeks — and it would only have made the wrong model easier to use.

Chosen

Rebuild the data model

A much harder technical lift, and the only foundation that matches how law actually works — or that reliable AI could be built on.

What made it difficult

Legacy contracts don't declare their own relationships

An amendment could modify an MSA while sitting in a folder beneath an SOW. The filing location frequently contradicted the legal effect.

Some relationships can be inferred; others can't

Extraction gets you part of the way. Where documents genuinely conflict or stay silent, a human has to confirm — so the model needed a verified state, not just an inferred one.

Scale is lopsided

A single master agreement might govern hundreds of execution agreements. Any view had to survive one-to-many at that ratio without becoming a wall.

It had to coexist with the repository already in use

Enterprise customers were live on the folder model. The new structure had to be adopted alongside it, not in place of it on day one.

The model

Agreements became entities. Relationships became typed and explicit — governs, modifies, applies to, supersedes. Documents stopped being the structure and became the evidence attached to it.

Authority first

The governing agreement anchors the page and is never buried. Everything else is arranged beneath what governs it.

Applicability on the face

Each amendment states what it applies to — "applies to 5 SOWs" — so the answer is read, not reconstructed.

Supersession visible

"Replaces 2 amendments," and superseded items carry that status rather than quietly looking current.

Evidence attached

Documents hang off the record they prove, instead of being the thing you navigate.

The structure isn't a feature sitting on top of the AI.
It's the ground the AI stands on.

What it changed for AI

Without explicit relationships, a model asked "which amendment applies to the Boston SOW?" has to infer applicability every time — from retrieved documents, metadata and clause text. That inference can be good. It can't easily be shown, and a user who can't verify an answer eventually stops relying on it.

With human-verified relationships in place, applicability becomes an explicit traversal with traceable evidence: the SOW, its applied amendment edge, the amendment — plus who confirmed that relationship and when. The answer arrives with its provenance attached.

Does the structure even need to be visible?

A fair challenge: if the AI answers reliably, why show anyone a relationship graph? The answer isn't binary — it's a question of how much structure to surface, and when.

The position I argued for: AI answers need to be backed by visible structure, not replaced by it. When the assistant says "Amendment 4 applies to your Boston SOW," the user's next move is to verify — so the structure view becomes the footnote to every answer.

Three cases make that permanent rather than transitional: people who need to verify before signing off, people who need to correct a relationship the system inferred, and people who need to govern a portfolio rather than query it one contract at a time. Accountability requires visibility.

One interaction detail I obsessed over+

On the graph canvas, a bucket holding 145 contracts and a single contract were drawn identically. The eye had no way to tell scaffolding from substance.

A group is a question. A record is a thing. A group has no ID, no paper, no signature, and disappears the moment you switch view. A record is a legal instrument that exists whether you're looking at it or not. They shouldn't share a silhouette.

No single cue survives every zoom level, so they stack — at any zoom, at least one is doing the work:

The same discipline drove scale: one agreement with 340 statements of work never renders as a wall. Each click opens the next column, only the opened branch is drawn, and no column holds more than about thirty records.

What changed

Before

Applicability was reconstructed by hand — 15–45 minutes per review, or a question asked of whoever remembered.

After

Governing agreement, applicable amendments and supersession are stated on the record itself. The question is read, not researched.

Business

Internal target of roughly 40% less review effort, and the elimination of conflicting "current" versions by design rather than by cleanup.

For AI

Applicability moved from something inferred on every question to something verified once and cited thereafter.

On the numbers: the redesign is recent, so post-launch measurement isn't in yet. The 40% is the target we designed against, not a result I've measured — I'd rather show the target than dress an estimate up as an outcome. Customer and colleague names are withheld; research came from logged escalations, a feedback review and a customer working session.

What I took from it

The most valuable thing I did here wasn't a screen. It was refusing the framing everyone arrived with. Ten problem reports all pointed at search, and search was never the answer — but that's only visible if you're willing to work on the model underneath instead of the interface on top.

What a system can represent sets the ceiling on what any model built over it can understand.

Let's build something that thinks.
