Two decisions I helped change

Both of these were worked out with roughly 3 to 4 designers, 2 to 3 product managers and 4 to 5 engineers, plus legal subject-matter experts. Neither was mine to decide alone.

01The product modelled where documents sit. I argued it had to model how contracts relate.

Context

A parent document with children beneath it. Embedded in the product, standard across the category, and asked for by customers by name.

The problem

An amendment can govern several agreements, and a record can inherit from more than one instrument. Those relationships are many to many, which a tree structure cannot represent.

Why it mattered

Get the model wrong and a user can misread their own contractual position. Any AI reasoning built on top of it inherits that error. Once this was a risk question rather than a preference, the argument got much shorter.

Outcome

Navigation separated from the relationship model: a flat list to move around in, explicit typed relationships underneath.

The full argument, and how it was made →

02The team was designing one surface at a time. I handed them three questions instead, and it changed the order they built in.

Context

Each surface treated as its own problem: design the chat, design the listing, design the procedure page. Nobody could settle which to build first.

What I proposed

Three questions to ask of any surface instead: who starts the interaction, what the person is trying to do, and which surface should carry it.

Why it mattered

Most AI features end up in a chat window out of habit, not because anyone decided they should. These questions made that a decision again.

Outcome

Listing and procedure were reclassified as discovery, which settled which to build first. Other designers then asked the same three questions of the listing, procedure and homepage work. To stop the language drifting back, I wrote the team's glossary for agentic contract work, around sixty terms, and still maintain it.

How I work

I design AI products, and I use AI to make them. Mostly so I can try more directions, and get something running in a browser while it is still early enough to change.

01 Prototype

Prototypes, not mockups

I build in HTML rather than linking static frames together. A branching graph holding 340 records behaves nothing like it does in a design tool. The only way to find out is to load the data and look at it.

  • family-graph-at-scale.html Does a many-to-many web stay readable at full volume?
  • node-language-concept.html Can relationships replace folders as the storage model?

02 Measure

Measure it, don't argue about it

My first relationship layout was radial, because that is what these always look like. I didn't argue for it. I instrumented it, ran it at full volume, and let the frame times end the discussion.

04 Judge

The part AI can't do

Plausible is cheap now. Which of them is actually right, what to cut, what I would not want to stand behind in a review: that part is still mine.

I removed several numbers from this site because I could not source them properly.

What I keep coming back to

Being sure and being allowed are two different things.

An agent can be completely sure of the answer and still have no business acting on it. The model owns the confidence. Somebody else owns the consequences. Most of what I design sits in the gap between those two.

About

What I have been trying to do at Sirion is make complexity feel calm. Legal teams already carry the weight of getting things exactly right. They shouldn't have to work out an interface on top of that.

I came to product design the long way round. Years in brand and creative direction, and I co-founded a denim label before moving into digital products. It shows up in the work. I care about how something reads as much as how it functions, and I would rather prove an idea in a browser than in a deck.

Being a father changed how I look at this. I watch my daughter work out how something new behaves. Where she gets stuck, what she tries next. That is the same thing I am watching for at work. Design is doing its job when it gets out of the way and the person just carries on.
